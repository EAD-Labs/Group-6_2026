Executive summary

School teachers need practical, safe ways to use generative AI but have little time for theory. PromptShala, built for the Centre for Educational Technology, IIT Bombay, is a self-paced web course with four modules and 29 lessons. Teachers write classroom prompts, receive CRAFT feedback, test reusable helpers and check AI drafts against sources. The client team used it in a teacher-training session on 7 October 2026; logs cover 31 teacher accounts, 24 of them new to AI. Teachers needed direction to find the practice lab, then engaged: 22 opened it, but only 3 completed a lesson and none opened Module 4. Among 9 teachers who changed their prompt under one evaluator, the median score rose from 60% to 80%, an early signal rather than proof of learning. The client valued real-time practice and plans further use, but asked for easier navigation, shorter routes and for features built specifically around teachers.

Understanding of requirement and context

The client's problem

CET asked for a platform that takes school teachers from knowing about AI to actually using it in their work. In the kickoff (7 Aug) the client stressed that teachers are beginners. In the following review (14 Aug) the scope was set as how to use AI (prompting, reusable assistants, working with sources), not AI theory. The stated demo goal was that a teacher could interact with the platform for about 30 minutes and leave knowing how to use AI in their work.

A workshop can explain these skills, but it rarely gives each teacher feedback on their own attempt. The product therefore centres on feedback: a teacher writes a prompt, the system shows which parts are missing against a visible rubric, and the teacher revises it.

Operational domain

The setting is teacher professional development, with teachers learning alongside their normal preparation work. As the client review of 28 Aug emphasised, teaching AI to teachers differs from teaching it to other professionals:

DimensionSchool teacherOther professional (banker, architect)
Who receives the outputChildren with varied age, prior knowledge and needsAdult clients, colleagues or regulators
Typical outputLesson plan, explanation, quiz, worksheet, parent messageFinancial analysis, specification, report
How quality is judgedAge fit, pedagogy, curriculum fit, misconceptions, teacher checkCommercial, regulatory or technical accuracy
Safety boundaryNo identifiable student data; the teacher stays responsibleDomain confidentiality and professional controls

Core system objectives

Learning moduleLessonsWhat the teacher should be able to do
1. AI foundations and responsible use6Judge when AI is suitable, verify output, protect privacy
2. Classroom prompt writing7Write and revise prompts using CRAFT (Context, Role, Action, Format, Target)
3. Reusable teacher assistants (AI Staffroom)8Set up, test, repair and hand over a reusable helper
4. Working with teacher-owned sources8Turn permitted material into a draft and check its claims against the source

The system must also grade quizzes on the server, save progress across devices, issue verifiable certificates, give staff cohort reports without exposing private prompts, and keep learning usable when the AI service fails.

Revision of prompts

16 teachers checked more than one prompt (median 5 checks; first to last in about 5 minutes). The evaluator varied: 66 of 79 checks used the rule-based checker and 13 used Gemini, and Gemini scored lower on average (44% against 63%). We therefore report only the 9 teachers who changed their prompt and had the same evaluator on first and last attempts: median 60% to 80%; 5 improved, 3 unchanged, 1 lower.

Qualitative

The client reported that teachers took longer to understand the system, but all began work once told to go to the CRAFT lab, and at least 15 used it to complete the problem. Teachers enjoyed seeing responses in real time.

Key insights and takeaways

  1. Teachers name the task but not the output or the learner. Action and Context scored high; Format and Target scored lowest. This supports the design of feedback by dimension.
  2. Teachers needed direction to find practice. They started once told to go to the CRAFT lab, so the 22 who opened it reflect that instruction as well as interest. The lab should be easier to reach.
  3. Session length and course length do not match. A 12-minute median cannot cover an 18-hour course, and only 3 of 31 completed a lesson; the client raised the same concern. Modules 3 and 4 were effectively not field-tested.
  4. AI availability is a real constraint. Most checks used the rule-based fallback, so practice continued, but scores varied by evaluator. The export does not record why, and device type was not captured. The client also asked for better logging.

Changes from the original persona. Our design document (HLD) assumed mainly Mathematics or Science teachers of Classes 5-10. The pilot group was more varied in subject, and most taught Classes 8-10, so examples should not rely on one subject.

Client critique of the persona work. In the review meeting, Prof. Rajendran asked what exists specifically for teachers and not for other users, such as buttons to select a class or subject directly. He said that keeping the teacher in mind "was not coming out clearly". The course is written for teachers, but its controls are not yet teacher-specific, and this page should be read with that gap in mind.

Secondary persona: Anil, teacher educator. Anil runs teacher-development programmes. He needs cohort progress and common difficulties, without routine access to teachers' private prompts or materials.

Design rationale for the persona

Persona need or constraintDesign decisionWhy it fits
New to AI; put off by technical termsPlain-language task names, classroom examples and short steps (simplified after client feedback on 9 Oct)Teachers can act without learning vocabulary first. The client later found the redesign harder to navigate (see gap below)
Accepts first output; omits format and learnerCRAFT feedback by dimension, with attempts side by sideShows exactly what to fix; the pilot confirms these gaps
Fears wrong informationClaim-to-source checks, teacher review steps, and labels showing live or rule-based feedbackKeeps judgement with the teacher and makes AI limits visible
Must protect student dataSafe-use notice before AI; private owner-only records; staff reports exclude prompt textLimits exposure and builds trust
Short, interrupted sessionsAll modules open (client-approved change from locked order); saved drafts; resume on any deviceTeachers start where the need is, and can stop safely
No paid AI accountCourse Gemini key held on the server, optional personal key kept only in the open tab, rule-based fallbackPractice works without setup, and no key reaches the browser bundle or database

Technology choices. Next.js 16 and React 19 give one responsive codebase (checked at 360, 768 and 1440 px). Server routes keep AI keys and grading away from the browser. Supabase Auth and PostgreSQL row-level security enforce privacy in the database itself, not only in the interface.

Known design gaps. Client review found that adding many modules made the CRAFT lab harder to find, that the experience feels like a structured course, and that there are no teacher-specific shortcuts such as class and subject selection. These are in the roadmap on page 5.

System architecture and data flow

Flow of a prompt check: (1) the teacher submits task and prompt; (2) the server checks sign-in, consent, rate limit and size; (3) Gemini evaluates it, or the rule-based checker does if Gemini fails; (4) the server validates scores and computes the total; (5) the attempt is saved under owner-only access and returned, labelled with its source.

Core features and edge case handling

Core modules: course and lessons; CRAFT practice with history; AI Staffroom; Source Studio and own-source workspace; server-graded quizzes; certificates with public verification; staff tools for cohorts, content and reports; account export and deletion; public /try and /review-lab tools.

Failure or edge caseHandling
Gemini fails, times out or returns malformed outputCRAFT returns rule-based feedback and source drafts fall back to extraction, both clearly labelled; the helper shows an unavailable message; the draft is kept
Too many AI requests, or the quota service failsHTTP 429 with a retry time; if quota checks fail, no paid call is made and the fallback is used
Poor connection or two devices editingLocal drafts, sync status with retry, and revision conflict handling
Duplicate or stale quiz submissionGraded on the server; duplicates are idempotent and stale submissions are rejected
No consent, or wrong roleAI blocked until the safe-use notice is accepted (403); learners cannot reach staff APIs
Private material kept too longSaved sources expire after 30 days; audit and activity records are cleaned after 90; users can delete at any time

Client validation and feedback summary

Reviews on 7, 14, 21 and 28 Aug set the persona, the focus on practical use and the CRAFT feedback. After the 9 Oct meeting the team simplified the interface, added questions on AI use, allowed a personal Gemini key and began saving CRAFT requests. The client approved open modules and public signup.

Field test. The client team used the platform in a teacher-training session. Teachers took time to understand the system, then at least 15 worked in the CRAFT lab once directed to it.

What worked (A. N. Rajmane, video)

  • "It's a really helpful app"; "We are happy to use this app"
  • Teachers "really enjoyed because they could see their responses in real time"
  • "For us it is really helpful because we get to train a lot of teachers"

What needs work (Prof. Rajendran, meeting; Rajmane, video)

  • "The UI can be better", and better logging is expected
  • The redesign was "even more complicated" and the lab took time to find
  • The UX "feels too much like a very structured course"; teachers may lack time for all of it

Acceptance status. The client is positive and reports continuing use, expecting "minor fixes" through iteration, and asked for no product changes before submission. There is no formal sign-off: the 19-case acceptance sheet is unfilled.

Technical verification (team, test accounts). 435 automated tests and 27 browser journeys passed on 15 Oct. A live production check on 23 Oct passed core flows and all three Gemini features. This shows that the system works, not that it helps teachers.

Value proposition. PromptShala gives CET a repeatable way to give each teacher individual feedback on their own prompts, which a single workshop facilitator cannot do at the same scale, while keeping teacher judgement and student privacy central. The pilot supports only the first step of this claim.
